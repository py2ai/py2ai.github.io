When Google published "BERT: Pre-training of Deep Bidirectional Transformers for Language Understanding" in October 2018, the paper landed together with a reference implementation — the `google-research/bert` repository. That repo is the artifact this tour reads. It is written for TensorFlow 1.x in graph mode, built on the Estimator API, and it carries the unmistakable texture of late-2018 research code: flag parsing at the top of every script, `TPUEstimator` wiring, and comments that explain not just what the code does but why the authors chose that design.

What strikes most readers first is the size. There is no package directory, no `src/` tree, no build system. The whole thing is roughly a dozen files at the repository root, and the ones that matter are eight Python scripts: `modeling.py`, `tokenization.py`, `run_classifier.py`, `run_squad.py`, `optimization.py`, `extract_features.py`, `create_pretraining_data.py`, and `run_pretraining.py`. That is the complete surface area of the most influential language model of its era — the codebase that other teams ported to PyTorch, that spawned the fine-tuning ecosystem, and that turned "pre-train once, fine-tune everywhere" from an idea into an industry default.

Reading it top-down is rewarding precisely because the graph-mode style forces every tensor transformation to be explicit. Nothing hides behind an `nn.Module` with implicit state; you can trace a token from a raw string all the way to a probability without leaving a handful of files. This post walks that path at two zoom levels, mapped by the two diagrams below.

Reading the overview from left to right:

- [BertModel, L107](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/modeling.py#L107) is the heart of the repo. Its constructor wires the embedding lookup, the attention mask, the encoder stack, and a tanh pooler into one computation graph, then exposes accessors like `get_sequence_output()` and `get_pooled_output()` for downstream heads.
- [BertConfig, L31](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/modeling.py#L31) is the hyperparameter record that separates BERT-Base (12 layers, 768 hidden units, 12 heads) from BERT-Large (24 layers, 1024 hidden, 16 heads), including dropout rates and the GELU activation choice.
- [FullTokenizer, L161](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/tokenization.py#L161) is the single entry point for raw text. It chains a rule-based cleaner with a subword splitter and finally maps tokens to vocabulary indices.
- [WordpieceTokenizer, L300](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/tokenization.py#L300) implements the greedy longest-match search that turns words missing from the vocabulary into `##`-prefixed fragments — the detail that let a 30k-token vocabulary cover English nearly losslessly.
- [model_fn_builder, L619](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_classifier.py#L619) returns the `model_fn` closure that `TPUEstimator` calls; it is the junction where the encoder, the task head, the loss, and the training op meet.
- [create_optimizer, L25](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/optimization.py#L25) assembles the learning-rate schedule — linear warmup followed by polynomial decay — and clips gradients to a global norm of 1.0.
- [AdamWeightDecayOptimizer, L87](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/optimization.py#L87) is the Adam variant with decoupled weight decay, deliberately skipping LayerNorm kernels and biases — a recipe later papers copied almost verbatim.
- [write_predictions, L741](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_squad.py#L741) closes the question-answering loop, joining raw start/end logits with token features to emit n-best answer strings in original text.

## One File, One Encoder: modeling.py

Everything in `modeling.py` exists to answer one question: given `input_ids`, `input_mask`, and `token_type_ids`, what tensors come out? The constructor of `BertModel` reads like a checklist of the architecture. First, [embedding_lookup, L380](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/modeling.py#L380) maps integer WordPiece ids to dense vectors, with a one-hot matmul path kept for TPU efficiency. Then [embedding_postprocessor, L428](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/modeling.py#L428) adds learned position embeddings and segment (token-type) embeddings on top, applies LayerNorm and dropout — this is where BERT differs from the original Transformer by using *learned* positions rather than sinusoids.

Attention itself is split into two functions. [attention_layer, L558](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/modeling.py#L558) does one round of multi-head scaled dot-product attention: it reshapes the 768-dim hidden states into per-head slices, computes `Q·Kᵀ/√d`, adds the mask as an additive bias before the softmax, and projects the concatenated heads back to the model width. [transformer_model, L754](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/modeling.py#L754) is the loop that runs `num_hidden_layers` rounds of that attention plus a position-wise feed-forward network activated by [gelu, L264](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/modeling.py#L264). The attention mask starts life as a simple 2D padding indicator and is expanded into a 3D bias by [create_attention_mask_from_input_mask, L524](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/modeling.py#L524).

Finally, the pooler. Lines 224 through 232 squeeze out the hidden state of the first token (the `[CLS]` slot), push it through a dense tanh layer, and stash the result; [get_pooled_output, L234](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/modeling.py#L234) hands it to task heads, while `get_sequence_output()` returns the full per-token sequence for tagging and extraction.

## WordPiece, With the Rules in Plain Sight: tokenization.py

The tokenizer file is the most portable code in the repo — pure Python, no TensorFlow at all. [load_vocab, L121](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/tokenization.py#L121) reads `vocab.txt`, one token per line, into an ordered dictionary. [FullTokenizer, L161](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/tokenization.py#L161) then composes two stages. Stage one is [BasicTokenizer, L185](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/tokenization.py#L185): optional lower-casing and accent stripping, splitting CJK characters into singletons, and breaking punctuation into separate tokens. Stage two is [WordpieceTokenizer, L300](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/tokenization.py#L300): for each word, take the longest prefix that exists in the vocabulary, mark it, then repeat on the remainder with a `##` continuation marker, giving up after 100 characters and emitting the `[UNK]` token instead.

The output mapping happens in [convert_tokens_to_ids, L144](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/tokenization.py#L144), a trivial dictionary lookup that nonetheless defines the exact integer contract every downstream consumer depends on. Because the logic is deterministic and dependency-free, this file was lifted almost unchanged into later reimplementations; its modern, fast-Rust descendant lives on in [tokenizers](/tokenizers/), which still ships BERT's original vocab format.

## Two Task Scripts, One Pattern

`run_classifier.py` and `run_squad.py` share an identical skeleton: read examples, convert to fixed-length features, build a model on top of `BertModel`, wrap it in an Estimator, train, predict. Learning one teaches you the other, and both teach you the TF1 idiom.

In the classifier script, GLUE datasets enter through `DataProcessor` subclasses ([MrpcProcessor, L296](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_classifier.py#L296), [XnliProcessor, L207](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_classifier.py#L207)) that yield `InputExample` records. [convert_single_example, L377](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_classifier.py#L377) tokenizes a sentence pair and calls `_truncate_seq_pair` to fit `max_seq_length` by trimming the longer sentence from the right; [file_based_convert_examples_to_features, L479](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_classifier.py#L479) serializes the [InputFeatures, L161](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_classifier.py#L161) records into TFRecord protos that [file_based_input_fn_builder, L509](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_classifier.py#L509) parses back inside the Estimator input function. The head is deliberately boring: [create_model, L574](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_classifier.py#L574) takes the pooled output, applies dropout, and does a single matrix multiply into a softmax.

The SQuAD script adds span logic. [read_squad_examples, L227](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_squad.py#L227) aligns answer character offsets to whitespace tokens; [convert_examples_to_features, L309](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_squad.py#L309) slides a `doc_stride` window across long passages, marking which window holds the true answer via `_check_is_max_context`; the model at [create_model, L550](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_squad.py#L550) emits two logits vectors for start and end positions. Prediction time in [write_predictions, L741](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_squad.py#L741) reassembles WordPiece output into readable text with [get_final_text, L927](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_squad.py#L927). A third runner, `extract_features.py` (entry at [main, L343](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/extract_features.py#L343)), skips heads entirely and dumps contextual embeddings from any requested layer — still a popular way to get fixed BERT features for downstream models.

## From Install to Your First Model

This is TensorFlow 1-era code, and honesty matters here: the paths below describe how the repo was meant to be run, and what it costs. The `requirements.txt` asks for `tensorflow >= 1.11.0` (CPU build; the GPU build was a separate `tensorflow-gpu` package). On a modern machine the pragmatic route is a dedicated Python 3.7-or-earlier environment with the last TF1 release:

```shell
conda create -n bert-tf1 python=3.7 -y
conda activate bert-tf1
pip install tensorflow==1.15
git clone https://github.com/google-research/bert.git
python run_classifier.py --help   # sanity-check the flag surface
```

Then download the `uncased_L-12_H-768_A-12` BERT-Base checkpoint, grab the GLUE MRPC data, and run the README's fine-tuning example:

```shell
export BERT_BASE_DIR=/path/to/uncased_L-12_H-768_A-12
export GLUE_DIR=/path/to/glue

python run_classifier.py \
  --task_name=MRPC \
  --do_train=true \
  --do_eval=true \
  --data_dir=$GLUE_DIR/MRPC \
  --vocab_file=$BERT_BASE_DIR/vocab.txt \
  --bert_config_file=$BERT_BASE_DIR/bert_config.json \
  --init_checkpoint=$BERT_BASE_DIR/bert_model.ckpt \
  --max_seq_length=128 \
  --train_batch_size=32 \
  --learning_rate=2e-5 \
  --num_train_epochs=3.0 \
  --output_dir=/tmp/mrpc_output/
```

MRPC is tiny — about 3,600 examples — so this converges in roughly 343 steps and a few minutes on most GPUs, landing near 84–88% dev accuracy. The README is candid about hardware: everything runs on CPU, GPU, or Cloud TPU; BERT-Base fine-tuning fits comfortably in 12 GB of GPU memory, while BERT-Large wants 12–16 GB and will still out-of-memory on SQuAD even at batch size 1 on a 12 GB card. Pretraining from scratch is another world entirely — the paper's BERT-Base replication took about an hour on a 16-cloud-TPU pod, and `run_pretraining.py` (entry at [main, L406](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/run_pretraining.py#L406)) assumes that scale, with `create_pretraining_data.py` ([main, L436](https://github.com/google-research/bert/blob/master/create_pretraining_data.py#L436)) masking 15% of tokens up front.

## Honest Limits

Treat this repository as an archive, not a dependency. It is frozen TF1 code: `tf.layers`, `Estimator`, and `tf.contrib` calls that modern TensorFlow removed, plus Python 2/3 straddling helpers like `convert_to_unicode`. Nothing here has been maintained for years, and Google has long since moved on. The architecture itself was also superseded within two years — RoBERTa, ELECTRA, and DeBERTa each kept the skeleton and replaced the training recipe or the head design.

For real work today, the ecosystem lives elsewhere. Hugging Face [transformers](/transformers/) reimplements BERT in [PyTorch](/pytorch/), loads these exact checkpoints, and has become the de facto interface for the entire encoder family. The WordPiece algorithm survived as the [tokenizers](/tokenizers/) library in fast Rust. If you need the original frozen graph out of the TF1 world, exporting through [ONNX](/onnx/) remains the cleanest exit. What this repo still offers that no replacement can, is legibility: eight files where every design decision of the foundational era — learned positions, the `[CLS]` pooler, decoupled weight decay, warmup schedules — sits in plain sight with a comment above it.
